# Sonic taxonomy

Source: expanded v2 axes, with the original v1 emphasis on audible qualities.

## Era and sonic period

* Late 1960s–early 1970s: fuzz guitar, psychedelic experimentation, tape effects, progressive arrangements, unusual library recordings, electric folk transitions.
- Mid–late 1970s: analog polyphonic synthesizers, motorik rhythms, cosmic funk, Afro-rock, roots dub, exploratory disco, private-press AOR.
- Early–mid 1980s: DX7-era FM synthesis, LinnDrum/DMX/TR-808 percussion, slap or fretless bass, gated reverb, post-punk economy, cassette-culture DIY.
- Late 1980s–early 1990s: TB-303 acid basslines, pitched-down dance records, new beat, industrial swing, sample-based loops, street soul, early trip-hop.
- Post-Y2K & 2000s Underground: micro-house, blog-house, electroclash, lo-fi bedroom indietronica, minimal synth revivals.
- Pre-1975 Southeast Asian electric music: Saigon surf/soul/psych, Cambodian Khmer-language electric pop and separately documented Lao-era vocal/pop traditions.
- 1980s–1990s Memphis cassette rap: 808 lows, murky tape textures, minimalist loops and sometimes horror-tinged keys.
- 1990s–2000s Indonesian DIY punk/hardcore and Hindi-language band rock/folk fusion: different local scenes, not a single exotic-rock label.
- 2010s–2020s SoundCloud sigilkore: chopped vocals, bitcrushing, pitch/tempo shifts and saturated trap production (not a synonym for old Memphis tapes).
- Contemporary nonbinary country/Americana and Indigenous North American/Australian rock: cross-scene guitar/songwriting routes, not identity-derived instrument claims.

## Sonic textures and instrumentation

* Synths and electronics: Moog, ARP Odyssey, EMS VCS3, Oberheim, PPG Wave, Fairlight CMI stabs; analog sequencers and modular patches.
- Guitars and keys: spring-reverb surf/chicha guitar, fuzz-wah psych leads, angular no-wave scratch guitar, Farfisa/Vox Continental organ, Rhodes, Clavinet.
- Rhythm and bass: motorik 4/4 drums, TR-808/TR-909/DMX patterns, live hand percussion (timbales, congas, guacharaca), fretless/slap bass, heavy live breaks, steppers dub basslines.
- Recording atmosphere: Roland Space Echo and tape saturation, spring/plate reverb, vocoders and talkboxes, vinyl wear, cassette grit, cinematic space. Use these as audible features; do not claim an instrument appears on a particular track without confirming it.

## Operating guidance

1. Start from 2–4 **audible traits**, not a country flag or a genre tag.
2. Identify essential vs optional traits. A cross-scene suggestion must have a credible audible connection.
3. Specific production gear, instrumentation, tempos, and release dates are unconfirmed until supported by a reliable source; don't invent precision.
4. Follow the sound beyond the listed examples; don't make all playlists converge on the same reference artists.


## Mood interpretation matrix

These are listening hypotheses, never claims that an entire culture feels one way. Choose one main interpretation and a compatible secondary shade; vary them on the next open-ended request. User specifics and tempo win. A fast sad request can be cathartic; slow anger can be quietly menacing. Read the category index in global-scenes.md before defaulting to familiar examples.

| Mood | Interpretations to rotate | Avoid the shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Happy | communal celebration; mischievous play; private contentment; relief after hardship; romantic buoyancy; ecstatic release | major key, disco or fast automatically means happy |
| Angry | cold contempt; righteous protest; simmering resentment; betrayed intimacy; defiant solidarity; explosive catharsis | metal, shouting or higher tempo automatically means angry |
| Sad | homesickness; grief; resignation; heartbreak; lonely urban drift; bittersweet acceptance | slow piano, English breakup lyrics or minor key by default |
| Weird | unstable tuning; interlocking or asymmetric time; surreal narratives; homemade sound sources; radically edited form; uncanny restraint | unfamiliar language is weird; abrasive is always better |
| Funky | unhurried pocket; springy rhythmic tension; interlocking ensemble parts; sparse displaced accents; propulsive electronic interplay | American funk template, cheerful dance music or a funk tag automatically qualifies |
| Criminal | heists; smuggling; fugitives; criminal betrayal; prison escapes; criminal codes | prison, geography or artist reputation proves a crime narrative |

For Criminal, strongly prefer non-English vocals; occasional Jamaican Patois dancehall can offer a more English-accessible change of pace without assuming comprehension. Verify explicit crime at **song level**; see verification.md. Songs may boast, confess, mourn, warn, question or narrate. Rotate story angles: heists and criminal ingenuity; smuggling routes; fugitives and evasion; betrayal inside criminal networks; prison escapes; underworld codes and loyalty; and criminal bravado with its consequences. The selected angle is a discovery hypothesis, not a quota across twelve songs. Avoid twelve interchangeable tough-guy declarations. Criminal's documented narrative requirement outranks random discovery lenses and crate sparks; the user's tempo still applies. Keep the DeBarge exception intact.

## Peculiarity by audible mechanism

These are search categories, not invented microgenre names. Combine one or two with a real scene; never claim a technique or instrument without evidence.

| Family | Distinct search routes |
|---|---|
| Time | additive meter; interlocking pulses; lopsided swing; shifting accents; elastic rubato; stop-start silence |
| Pitch | beating close intervals; alternate tuning; modal ambiguity; sliding pitch; unresolved drones; detuned unisons |
| Voice | overlapping entrances; antiphonal response; spoken/sung friction; intimate unaccompanied voice; extended vocal technique; dense multipart singing |
| Material | prepared strings; homemade instruments; struck metal; breath and reeds; bowed overtones; small acoustic ensembles amplified harshly |
| Studio | tape-splice form; feedback networks; dub subtraction; granular fragments; deliberate digital clipping; dry close-miked detail |
| Structure | miniature song forms; patiently accumulating cycles; abrupt edits; deceptive repetition; instrumental negative space; acoustic/electronic call-and-response |
| Archive | regional radio sessions; small-label singles; cassette compilations with credited tracks; film cues outside famous scores; private-press songcraft; live community ensembles |

Archival scarcity is not musical merit. A compilation excerpt or radio collage is not automatically a standalone song; reject unverifiable credits. Preserve sacred, ceremonial and community context rather than treating it as spooky decoration. Never infer mood from language, nationality, religion or identity.

## Freshness procedure

1. Choose a mood interpretation, an audible mechanism and a discovery category before searching artist names. Compare three genuinely different possible theses, then use the strongest; do not print an internal scoring system.
2. For an unconstrained ordinary set, research beyond one label or compilation and beyond the named reference artists. Trace original releases, local contemporaries and collaborators. Do not swap twelve obvious compilation staples for twelve other staples from one compilation.
3. When recent lists are accessible, exclude the last up to three sets' normalized artist/title/version recordings and prefer different artists. Also vary the dominant scene, source compilation and sonic thesis. Reissues, alternate uploads, aliases and featured credits do not reset a repeated recording.
4. If a specific request needs an exception, honor it and explain briefly. No mandatory nation, language, era, label or genre quota; no arbitrary sacrifice of cohesion for obscurity.
5. When history is unavailable, diversify the research and avoid named-example defaults, but never claim listener-specific novelty or persistent memory. The PWA stores only discovery cue IDs, not the resulting songs or listening history.
6. When the listener says stale, replace the discovery strategy and actual selections, not merely adjectives, title or ordering. Ask for prior lists only if essential to a precise exclusion request; do not block an ordinary GO.


## Recording-level selection gate

Seek accessible pleasure with a fingerprint. A familiar hook is welcome; popularity, polished production and broad genre names are not automatic exclusions. Obscurity, unusual provenance, lo-fi sound and ironic framing are not quality certificates either.

Before accepting each ordinary selection, require:
- Fit with the listener's selected regions, mood and tempo. Treat a regional scene as a research lead, not proof of a song's suitability. For Criminal, verify the explicit crime narrative at song level; do not weaken it to fill a regional quota. If the non-English preference materially limits a requested region, be candid rather than silently escaping the geography.
- A concrete, supported musical reason: phrasing, arrangement, groove, melodic turn, texture or a persuasive transition. Generic genre adjectives do not suffice. Never invent listening evidence.
- A useful contribution to the sequence beyond novelty or a funny backstory. A pop reference must remain pleasurable without the joke; do not use an ironic excuse for filler.
- Search beyond predictable signature tracks and repeated compilation selections. Broader genres need narrower research angles, not blanket bans; known artists remain eligible when the recording earns its place or the user requests it.

Use the region-tagged discovery catalog as a set of research doors. Regional entries are starting hypotheses with recording-level verification still required. Geography-neutral musical lenses remain available in every region; geographically specific lenses require a compatible selected region. With no region selection, the full worldwide pool is eligible. Local scenes and diaspora links must be independently documented for a specific release; language and artist identity alone do not establish the connection.
